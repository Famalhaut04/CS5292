The Spine of This Lecture
Audit vs. Compliance, in One Screen
Compliance
A state. Either you meet a standard — a law, a regulator’s rule, a contract, a framework you chose to adopt — or you don’t.
Mandatory (law, regulator) or voluntary (adopted for market trust). Either way: the target.
Audit
The independent verification that the compliant state is real.
Three words carry the definition: independence, evidence, opinion.
A control can be designed correctly — would it work, on paper — without operating correctly: did it actually work, in practice, over time. Every framework in this lecture tests both halves.

Framework Deep Dive
ISO/IEC 27001:2022
History
Traces back to a 1998 British Standard (BS 7799) written by the UK’s BSI. Adopted internationally as ISO/IEC 27001 in 2005, revised in 2013 to align with ISO’s common management-system structure, and revised again in 2022 — the current edition, which reduced Annex A from 114 to 93 controls and reorganized them into four themes.
What it covers
An Information Security Management System (ISMS) — the people, processes, and technology an organization uses to manage information-security risk, not just a technical checklist. The 93 Annex A controls span four themes: Organizational, People, Physical, and Technological.
Who typically pursues it
Organizations that need to prove security to customers, regulators, or partners internationally — especially outside the US, where it’s often a bid requirement or contractual expectation. Over 70,000 certificates have been issued worldwide across 150+ countries.

Framework Deep Dive
SOC 2
History
Introduced by the AICPA (American Institute of Certified Public Accountants) in 2010, under auditing standard SSAE 16, as a successor to SAS 70, which had focused narrowly on financial-reporting controls. SOC originally stood for “Service Organization Controls”; in 2017 (SSAE 18) the AICPA renamed it “System and Organization Controls,” keeping the same abbreviation.
What it covers
Five Trust Services Criteria: security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy. A CPA firm tests against whichever criteria the org selects (security is mandatory). Type I reports on control design at a point in time; Type II adds operating effectiveness over 6–12 months.
Who typically pursues it
Overwhelmingly US-based technology, SaaS, and cloud-hosting companies. It’s not a legal requirement — it’s contractual, driven by enterprise customers who won’t sign a vendor contract without seeing a current report.

Framework Deep Dive
CIS Controls v8
History
Began in 2008 as the SANS Top 20 / Critical Security Controls, built by a grassroots, international consortium of practitioners. Ownership transferred to the Center for Internet Security (CIS) in 2015. Version 8, released May 2021, consolidated 20 controls into 18 and shifted focus from device-centric to data-centric security.
What it covers
18 controls broken into 153 specific Safeguards, organized into three Implementation Groups (IG1, IG2, IG3) so an organization can prioritize by size and resources — IG1 is defined as essential cyber hygiene, the minimum baseline for every enterprise.
Who typically pursues it
Organizations wanting a free, practical, prioritized starting point without commissioning a formal paid audit — often smaller or mid-sized companies, or larger ones using it as a maturity roadmap. Frequently mapped into other regulatory and contractual frameworks rather than pursued as a standalone credential.

Worth Correcting in Class
Certified, Not Accredited
What actually happens
A national body — Hong Kong’s Hong Kong Accreditation Service (HKAS), for instance — accredits certification bodies. Those accredited certification bodies then certify your organization against ISO 27001.
So the precise phrase is “ISO 27001‑certified,” not “accredited” — though “accredited” is common enough informal usage that it’s worth naming the mix-up out loud rather than silently using the right word yourself.
A companion mix-up worth flagging
SOC 2 doesn’t use either word — it’s a CPA opinion, not a certificate. “SOC 2 certified” is a common but technically incorrect phrase — you’ll hear it from vendors who should know better.

The Common Thread
None of Them Test Every Transaction
A certification auditor, a CPA doing SOC 2 Type II, and an org scoring itself against CIS are doing the same underlying thing — testing whether controls are designed and operating effectively. All three need a defensible way to sample.
The nuance worth naming
Assurance level drives sample size.
A certification surveillance audit, a SOC 2 Type II, and an internal CIS self-check don’t need the same depth of evidence — even for the identical control.
What follows is the practical toolkit: how items get picked, two reference tables for how much to pick, and four ways to gather the evidence itself.

Gathering the Evidence
Four Techniques, Rising Assurance
Inquiry
Ask the control owner how it works. Necessary, never sufficient alone.
Observation
Watch the control being performed, once, in real time.
Inspection
Examine documents or system records the control produced.
Re-performance
Independently redo the control yourself and compare the outcome.
Inquiry/observation mostly establish design; inspection/re-performance over a period prove operating effectiveness — the SOC 2 Type I / Type II split is exactly this line, drawn in one framework.

Annex A, 2022 Edition
A.8.8 — Management of Technical Vulnerabilities
What it requires
Timely identification and remediation of technical vulnerabilities — the standard’s own guidance names vendor alerts, automated scanners, and penetration tests as valid identification methods under this one control.
Why it’s on this list
The single most repeated technical root cause behind real breaches: known, internet-facing vulnerabilities left unpatched for months to years despite vendor advisories.
Evidence to recommend requesting
• Credentialed vulnerability scan reports for the sampled period, scope reconciled against the asset inventory
• Patch deployment tickets showing remediation timeliness against the org’s own Service Level Agreement (SLA)
• The most recent penetration test / red team report — full scope, findings, and retest evidence
• Risk-acceptance documentation for anything still open past SLA
Sample scales with cadence: quarterly scans → 2 of 4; monthly patch cycle → 3–5 of 12; annual pentest → 1 (the full engagement).

Annex A, 2022 Edition
A.8.5 — Secure Authentication
What it requires
Authentication proportionate to risk. ISO doesn’t name Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) by number, but contemporary audits treat it as de facto required for remote/VPN access and privileged or admin accounts.
Why it’s on this list
The single most-cited technical finding in real-world security audits: no multi-factor authentication on remote access or administrative accounts.
Evidence to recommend requesting
• Current Identity Provider (IdP) / VPN policy export showing MFA is enforced, not merely available
• Who can change that policy, plus change-management approval records
• One live test: a non-MFA login attempt must be rejected
System-enforced control — sample 1. The IT General Control (ITGC) around the policy (who can change it, and whether they did) is what widens the test, not the volume of logins.

Annex A, 2022 Edition
A.8.2 — Privileged Access Rights
What it requires
Admin/root access kept separate from everyday accounts, individually attributable (no shared logins), granted on least privilege, and formally requested and approved.
Why it’s on this list
A recurring root cause in real incidents: dormant privileged accounts left active for years, credentials unrotated, no tiering between remote-login and domain-wide privileges.
Evidence to recommend requesting
• Current inventory of every account holding admin/domain-admin/root rights, with business justification
• Access-request and approval tickets for a sampled set of grants
• The most recent periodic privileged-access review, with evidence stale accounts were actually removed
• Privileged-activity logs tying specific actions to a named individual
Sample the most recent review cycle (1), plus a haphazard pull of 3–5 individual privileged accounts to trace back to an approval.

Annex A, 2022 Edition
A.5.35 — Independent Review of Information Security
What it requires
A periodic, independent check that the ISMS itself — not just one control — is working as intended. ISO requires “planned intervals or when significant changes occur,” not a fixed cadence; the reviewer must be independent of the area under review.
Why it’s on this list
A recurring finding in practice: audits skipped for years, or scoped to exclude network infrastructure entirely — a governance failure, distinct from any one technical control not working.
Evidence to recommend requesting
• The audit programme document: scope, frequency, methodology — confirm network infrastructure is explicitly in scope
• A reviewer-independence declaration for whoever performed it
• The most recent review report and its full finding list
• The corrective-action log, with re-test evidence that fixes actually closed
Sample the most recent cycle (1). The test is as much about what was in scope as what was found.

Annex A, 2022 Edition
A.8.16 — Monitoring Activities
What it requires
A detective control: anomalous-behaviour monitoring against a defined baseline, with alerts reviewed and acted on. ISO doesn’t mandate 24/7 coverage or a named Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) tool — it mandates a process proportionate to risk.
Why it’s on this list
A recurring pattern across real incident write-ups: inadequate detection and alerting, and log-retention gaps that limited how much investigators could later reconstruct.
Evidence to recommend requesting
• Shift/triage logs across the period claimed, if 24/7 coverage is claimed
• Escalation timestamps — time from alert raised to analyst action
• Evidence a sample of alerts was actually reviewed, not auto-closed by volume
Frequency-scaled like a daily control: for a continuous-monitoring claim, sample roughly 30–40 days across shifts.

Before the Break
What to Take Into the Group Task
Compliance is the target; audit is the verification.
Every control you test has a design half and an operating half.
ISO 27001 certifies; SOC 2 attests; CIS benchmarks.
Three different mechanisms answering the same underlying question.
Sample size follows assurance level, not habit.
Frequency and confidence/tolerance both give you a defensible starting point.
A.8.8, A.8.5, A.8.2, A.5.35, A.8.16 aren’t abstractions.
Each one maps to a control failure that shows up again and again in real security incidents — know these five, and you know what an auditor will ask about first.
