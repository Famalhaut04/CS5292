Before We Start
Compliant — and Still Breached
In December 2013, Target Corporation disclosed the theft of roughly 40 million payment-card records and 70 million additional customer records — at the time, one of the largest retail breaches in US history.
The detail that makes this a compliance case, not just a security case
“As recently as September 2013, our systems were certified as compliant with the Payment Card Industry data-security standards.”
— Target CFO John Mulligan, public Congressional testimony
Two months before the breach, Target passed a PCI DSS audit. So the question for today isn’t just “what went wrong” — it’s what a passed audit was, and wasn’t, actually testing.

Verified Timeline — Source: US Senate “Kill Chain Analysis,” Mar. 2014
How the Breach Unfolded
Sep 2013
Phishing emails deliver Citadel malware to Fazio Mechanical Services (Target’s HVAC vendor) — free antivirus only, no real-time protection. Credentials stolen. (At least 2 months before the Nov 12 intrusion, per the Senate report.)
Sep 2013
Same window — Target’s own systems pass their PCI DSS compliance assessment.
12 Nov 2013
Stolen Fazio credentials used to reach Target’s network — first access, via a vendor billing portal.
15–28 Nov 2013
Malware first installed on a small number of POS terminals — lateral movement into more sensitive zones. A vendor-facing system should never have reached the cardholder-data environment.
28–30 Nov 2013
Symantec flags malicious behaviour (28th); by the 30th, the majority of Target’s POS systems are infected and exfiltration malware is installed.
2–3 Dec 2013
Malware starts sending stolen data to an external FTP server. FireEye fires its first urgent alerts the same day — nobody acts.
12 Dec 2013
US Department of Justice notifies Target of the breach — the first Target itself learns of it.
18–19 Dec 2013
Krebs on Security breaks the story (18th); Target publicly confirms ~40M card accounts exposed (19th). A further 70M personal records disclosed 10 Jan 2014.

Per the Senate Report
Four Named Root Causes
1. Weak third-party security
Fazio was inadequately secured, and Target had no separate trust tier for vendor network access.
2. Ignored detection alerts
FireEye and Symantec both flagged the intrusion in real time. The report frames this as an operational and procedural failure — not a technology gap.
3. Inadequate network segmentation
The vendor billing portal had a reachable path into the cardholder-data environment.
4. Missed exfiltration detection
The data leaving the network over two-plus weeks was also visible, and also not acted on.

What Each Control Actually Requires
Control Descriptions (1/2)
A.5.19
Information Security in Supplier Relationships
Processes and procedures shall be defined and implemented to manage the information security risks associated with the use of a supplier’s products or services.
A.5.20
Addressing Security Within Supplier Agreements
Relevant information security requirements shall be established and agreed with each supplier, based on the type of supplier relationship.
A.8.16
Monitoring Activities
Networks, systems, and applications shall be monitored for anomalous behaviour, and appropriate actions taken to evaluate potential information security incidents.
A.5.26
Response to Information Security Incidents
Information security incidents shall be responded to in accordance with documented procedures.
Paraphrased from ISO/IEC 27002:2022 implementation guidance for teaching purposes — verify exact wording against a licensed copy of the standard before citing it formally.

Over To You — Homework
Draft an RFI Against the Controls We Just Covered
For each control: what would you ask Target to produce, before the breach, to know whether the control was really in place and really working?
(a) Control design
What policy, standard, or documentation would prove the control was set up correctly — on paper — before you test anything?
(b) Operating effectiveness
What would you actually sample — and how much — to prove the control operated as designed, in practice, over time?
